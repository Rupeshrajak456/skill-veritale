## Example 1: The Norman Conquest of 1066

Request: "Tell me the story of the Battle of Hastings and how Harold died."

```
Scope: c. 1064 to c. 1070 | Mode: full | Audience: general readers | Length: medium
```

### Timeline

### The story

When Edward the Confessor died on 5 January 1066, he left no son, and England had a new king within a day. Harold Godwinson was crowned on 6 January, although the sources disagree about who placed the crown on his head. Norman writers link the ceremony with Archbishop Stigand, while John of Worcester names Ealdred of York.

Across the Channel, Duke William of Normandy claimed that Edward had promised the throne to him. Norman sources add that Harold himself had sworn to support William, perhaps in 1064, but no English source mentions the oath and its terms are unclear.

Harold's first test came from the north. On 20 September Harald Hardrada of Norway and Harold's exiled brother Tostig beat the northern earls at Fulford. Five days later Harold caught them at Stamford Bridge, where both Hardrada and Tostig were killed. A story added later to one copy of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle tells of a lone Norwegian who held the bridge with an axe, but nothing early supports it.

On 28 September William's army landed at Pevensey. Twelfth-century writers such as Wace say he fell as he stepped ashore and turned the fall into a good omen. It makes a fine scene, but nobody who was there wrote it down.

The two armies met near Hastings on 14 October, and by evening William had won. Nobody knows how many men fought, and modern estimates vary widely. William of Poitiers says the Normans pretended to flee to draw the English out of position, though some historians suspect that real panics were tidied into tactics afterwards. Harold died in the battle. Whether an arrow struck him in the eye is still argued over. The Bayeux Tapestry is unclear on the point, and the Carmen de Hastingae Proelio has knights cutting him down. The Waltham Chronicle, written in the twelfth century, says Edith Swan-neck found his body.

William was crowned at Westminster on Christmas Day 1066. Monks at Battle Abbey later said their high altar stood on the very spot where Harold fell, but that is the abbey's tradition and not something the early sources record. The doubtful points are gathered in the note below.

### What we are unsure about

- Harold swears an oath to support William's claim to the English throne (Disputed): Only Norman sources describe the oath. English sources are silent, and the year and terms are uncertain.
- The archbishop who crowned Harold (Disputed): Norman sources link the ceremony with Archbishop Stigand, while John of Worcester names Ealdred of York.
- The size of the two armies (Disputed): Medieval figures are not trusted, and modern estimates vary widely.
- The Normans use planned, feigned retreats (Disputed): William of Poitiers describes deliberate feigned flights. Some historians think real panics were later presented as tactics.
- Harold is killed by an arrow in the eye (Disputed): The Tapestry figure is unclear and was restored in the nineteenth century, and the Carmen describes knights cutting Harold down.
- A single Norwegian holds the bridge with an axe (Legend): The story was added to the Chronicle long after the battle and has no early support.
- William falls as he steps ashore and turns it into a good omen (Legend): First told by writers working about a century later. No eyewitness records it.
- Edith Swan-neck identifies Harold's body (Legend): Told only in a source written more than a century later.
- The high altar of Battle Abbey marks the spot where Harold fell (Legend): This is the abbey's own tradition. The early sources do not record the exact spot.

Labels: Verified = broad agreement; Disputed = sources disagree; Legend = late or traditional source only.

## Example 2: Ashoka and the Kalinga war

Request: "Explain the Kalinga war and how it changed Ashoka, for my history class."

```
Scope: c. 273 BCE to 1915 CE | Mode: full | Audience: students | Length: medium
```

### Timeline

| Date | Event | Label | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| c. 273 to 268 BCE | Ashoka, son of Bindusara, succeeds to the Mauryan throne | Verified | Ashoka's edicts; Puranas; Buddhist texts |
| c. 273 to 268 BCE | Four years pass between his accession and his consecration | Disputed | Sri Lankan chronicles (Dipavamsa, Mahavamsa) |
| undated | Ashoka kills ninety-nine brothers to take power | Legend | Sri Lankan chronicles, written centuries later |
| undated | Ashoka keeps a torture chamber known as 'Ashoka's hell' | Legend | Ashokavadana (c. second century CE) |
| c. 261 BCE | Ashoka conquers Kalinga, on the east coast, in the eighth year after his consecration | Verified | Major Rock Edict XIII |
| c. 261 BCE | Edict XIII states that 150,000 were carried away, 100,000 killed and many times that number died | Verified | Major Rock Edict XIII |
| c. 261 BCE | Those figures as an accurate count of the dead and deported | Disputed | Major Rock Edict XIII only |
| after c. 261 BCE | Ashoka records deep remorse for the suffering in Kalinga | Verified | Major Rock Edict XIII |
| after c. 261 BCE | The war turned Ashoka to Buddhism | Disputed | Minor Rock Edict I; modern scholarship |
| undated | A young monk converts Ashoka (Nigrodha or Samudra) | Legend | Sri Lankan chronicles; Ashokavadana |
| c. 257 BCE onwards | Edict XIII is left out at Dhauli and Jaugada in Kalinga; separate edicts to officials stand there instead | Verified | The inscriptions at Dhauli and Jaugada |
| c. 256 BCE | Officers of dhamma (dhamma-mahamattas) are appointed in his thirteenth regnal year | Verified | Major Rock Edict V |
| undated | Ashoka builds 84,000 stupas | Legend | Ashokavadana and later Buddhist texts |
| c. 250 BCE | A Third Buddhist Council meets at Pataliputra under Moggaliputta Tissa | Disputed | Sri Lankan Pali sources |
| c. 232 BCE | Ashoka dies | Verified | Puranas; Buddhist chronologies; modern scholarship |
| 1837 CE | James Prinsep deciphers the Brahmi script, making the edicts readable | Verified | Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal |
| 1915 CE | An inscription found at Maski names the king as Asoka, linking the 'Piyadasi' of the edicts with the Ashoka of the texts | Verified | Maski Minor Rock Edict |

### The story

Ashoka succeeded his father Bindusara on the Mauryan throne some time between about 273 and 268 BCE [Verified]. The Sri Lankan chronicles say four years passed before his consecration, but no other source confirms the gap [Disputed]. The same chronicles claim he killed ninety-nine brothers to take power [Legend], yet his own Rock Edict V mentions the households of his brothers. A later Buddhist text, the Ashokavadana, adds a torture chamber known as 'Ashoka's hell' [Legend].

In the eighth year after his consecration, around 261 BCE, Ashoka conquered Kalinga on the east coast [Verified]. We know this from his own words. Major Rock Edict XIII says that 150,000 people were carried away, 100,000 were killed and many times that number died [Verified]. These are round figures from a single royal source, so we cannot treat them as a real count [Disputed]. The same edict records his deep remorse for the suffering [Verified]. Whether the remorse was sincere is a question of interpretation.

Many books say the war turned Ashoka to Buddhism. Minor Rock Edict I says he had been a lay follower for over two and a half years before he became zealous, and historians disagree on how this fits with the war [Disputed]. Buddhist texts credit a young monk with his conversion: Nigrodha in the Sri Lankan chronicles, Samudra in the Ashokavadana [Legend].

Edict XIII was not carved at Dhauli and Jaugada, the sites in Kalinga itself. Two separate edicts addressed to officials stand there instead [Verified]. In his thirteenth regnal year he appointed officers of dhamma to spread his moral teaching [Verified]. Legends say he built 84,000 stupas [Legend], and Pali sources describe a Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra that the edicts never mention [Disputed]. Ashoka died around 232 BCE [Verified].

For centuries nobody could read his edicts. James Prinsep deciphered the Brahmi script in 1837 [Verified], and in 1915 an inscription at Maski named the king as Asoka, which confirmed that the 'Piyadasi' of the edicts was the Ashoka of the texts [Verified].

### What we are unsure about

- Four years pass between his accession and his consecration (Disputed): Only the Sri Lankan chronicles report the gap, and historians differ on whether it points to a real struggle for the throne.
- Those figures as an accurate count of the dead and deported (Disputed): They are round numbers from a single royal source, with no independent count to check them against.
- The war turned Ashoka to Buddhism (Disputed): Minor Rock Edict I says he was a lay follower for over two and a half years before he became zealous, and historians disagree on how this fits with the war.
- A Third Buddhist Council meets at Pataliputra under Moggaliputta Tissa (Disputed): Known only from Sri Lankan Pali sources. The edicts never mention it.
- Ashoka kills ninety-nine brothers to take power (Legend): Written centuries later, and Ashoka's own Rock Edict V mentions the households of his brothers.
- Ashoka keeps a torture chamber known as 'Ashoka's hell' (Legend): Found only in a Buddhist text written centuries after his death.
- A young monk converts Ashoka (Nigrodha or Samudra) (Legend): The texts name different monks, and both are much later Buddhist accounts.
- Ashoka builds 84,000 stupas (Legend): The number belongs to Buddhist legend and has no archaeological support.

Labels: Verified = broad agreement; Disputed = sources disagree; Legend = late or traditional source only.
